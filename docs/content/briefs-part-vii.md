## Part VII overview (rows 37–40)

Part VII puts the grammar from earlier parts to work in everyday problems, services, written messages and Sofia's own story. Plan one lesson per outline row, in order: `37`, `38`, `39`, `40`. Do not turn row 40 into a survey of new rules; it is the final integration lesson. Each lesson must stand alone in the ongoing Utrecht story.

### Known knowledge and boundaries

- By row 36, learners can use present and selected past forms, main-clause V2, inversion after a fronted element, verb-final *dat/omdat/als* clauses, subject relatives with *die/dat*, purpose *om … te*, and future meanings of the present, *gaan* and *zullen*. The [Part VI ledger](ledger-part-vi.md) records the exact depth.
- [21-b](ledger-part-iv.md#21-b-ik-haal-je-op-accepted-2026-10-19) already practices object pronouns for people and things, their position, and personal forms after prepositions. Row 37 extends that system; it must not reintroduce *me/mij … ze* as if they were new.
- [35](ledger-part-vi.md#35-als-ik-tijd-had-accepted-2026-10-19) already teaches hypothetical *zou/zouden + infinitive*. Row 37 applies *zou* to polite requests rather than reteaching the conditional paradigm.
- *Wat vind je ervan?* from 24-a is a fixed chunk; row 37 may unpack the limited *er + preposition* forms below. Reflexive verbs belong to row 38. New clause links and sustained written register belong to row 39. Row 40 consolidates rather than adding a new grammar system.
- Check new terms against all accepted lesson files and ledgers in Parts I–VI. Recycle known vocabulary freely in the text, but give a New words card only to a first introduction or a documented new sense. Follow [style-guide.md](style-guide.md#new-words) for placement and forms.
- Each lesson follows the [style guide's skeleton and feature checklist](style-guide.md#lesson-skeleton): at least 7 practice blocks and 45 items, including a passage with at least six Dutch questions; two dialogues of 6–12 turns; at least ten translated examples; at least four callout variants; a column list, nested and numbered lists, dividers, 1–3 image ideas and no more than one link. Aim for 85–120 player steps and stay at or below 125. Every practice item needs an author's version and a useful note when a rule, alternative or common error matters.
- A fresh author and an independent reviewer handle each lesson. Work in batches of at most three lessons, finish review and validation before importing any lesson, and record accepted coverage before starting the next batch. The production course and manifest must be selected explicitly; never resend an existing production draft.

## Row 37 — Problems and asking for help

**Target:** apply known object pronouns to a practical problem; add a limited *hen/hun* distinction, useful *er + preposition* forms, independent possessives and polite *zou* requests.

**Story and continuity:** Wednesday 20 January 2027. Sofia asks Ahmed about a simple bicycle problem at his shop in the Kanaalstraat; she has owned the bike since 31 October. Ahmed and Sofia use *jij/je*. A second exchange may involve Henk helping Sofia identify which item belongs to whom, or a clearly marked formal phone role-play. Do not assert an expensive repair, an unsafe bicycle, or a new appointment.

**Grammar facts:**
- Quickly recycle the people/thing forms and pronoun position from 21-b in the context of a repair or borrowed item. Teach *hen* as a direct object or after a preposition, and *hun* as an indirect object without a preposition, with a few clear examples (*Ik zie hen*; *Ik geef hun de sleutel*; *Ik praat met hen*). Note that everyday speech often uses *ze*; do not imply *hen/hun* are the only natural choices or introduce *hun* as a subject.
- Teach only a small, productive *er + preposition* set useful here: *ermee* (with it), *ernaar* (at/to it) and *erover* (about it). Contrast with a person (*met hem/haar/hen*); explain that the preposition follows *er*. When a phrase is more natural with the noun repeated, repeat the noun. Recycle *ervan* from 24-a as a known chunk, not a fourth productive paradigm.
- Teach *van mij/jou* and *de/het mijne/jouwe* for simple ownership, with agreement to an omitted *de*- or *het*-word. Keep these forms in concrete situations; do not teach the full independent-possessive paradigm.
- Reuse *zou/zouden* from 35 for polite requests and offers: *Zou je ernaar kunnen kijken?*, *Zou u mij kunnen helpen?* and *Ik zou graag …* in a suitable formal exchange. Show a short, natural response. Keep verb order clear and do not claim *zou* is always required for politeness.

**Vocabulary:** roughly 15–25 genuinely new terms or chunks about a small problem, repair, ownership and requesting help. Check all earlier New words cards; *fiets*, *helpen*, *kunnen*, *zou/zouden* and already taught pronoun forms are recycled, not new cards.

**Practice:** choose the natural object form in context; contrast *hen/hun/ze*; replace a thing phrase with one of the three taught *er* forms; identify *de/het mijne/jouwe* from the omitted noun; rewrite a direct request with polite *zou*; word order, dialogue completion and translations both ways; a repair/help reading with at least six Dutch questions; one free-production request. Use grammar, warning, hint and culture/important callouts where they add a distinct rule or usage note.

**Off-limits:** a complete *er* paradigm (*daar-*, *waar-* and split *er … mee*), the whole independent-possessive table, object relative clauses, passive voice, and reflexive verbs before row 38. Keep real-world bicycle advice elementary.

## Row 38 — Doctor, municipality and services

**Target:** reflexive verbs in everyday and service contexts, and clear formal requests with *u*, *kunt u*, *wilt u* and recycled polite *zou*.

**Story and continuity:** Tuesday 26 January 2027, in Marieke's regular 19.00–21.00 class at the library in the centre. Marieke gives Sofia and Piotr two labelled role-plays: calling a doctor's office about a minor symptom and asking a municipal desk how to find or submit a form. The dialogues are simulated; neither character acquires an actual illness, appointment, registration problem or new legal status. Keep any service facts generic rather than claiming a specific Utrecht procedure.

**Grammar facts:**
- Introduce the common reflexive forms *me, je, zich, ons* with a bounded set of verbs: *zich voelen*, *zich wassen*, *zich aankleden* and *zich vergissen* as useful examples. Show the reflexive pronoun after the finite verb in a plain main clause and after the subject in an inverted clause; with an infinitive, place it with the infinitive group in a short, natural sentence. Do not present all reflexive verbs as interchangeable with non-reflexive verbs.
- Contrast *Ik voel me niet lekker* with the non-reflexive *Ik ben ziek* as situation language, without diagnosis or medical treatment advice. A vocabulary block may include symptom or service words only when introduced in a dialogue or explanation.
- Recycle *u* and formal/informal pairs from 01-a and 11-b. Practice a short request opening, the question itself, clarification and thanks: *Kunt u …?*, *Wilt u …?*, *Zou u … kunnen?*, *Alstublieft* and *Dank u wel*. Use formal *u* consistently with service staff; Marieke and classmates continue to use *jij/je* with one another.

**Vocabulary:** roughly 15–25 new useful words/chunks for symptoms, forms and service interactions, checked against Parts I–VI and row 37. Prefer transferable language (*de afspraak*, *het formulier*, *de balie*, if genuinely new) over invented local administrative details; verify each proposed card's first appearance.

**Practice:** choose/refit the reflexive pronoun, correct its placement, contrast reflexive and non-reflexive sentences, change an informal request into a formal one, complete a doctor/desk role-play, translations both ways, a short service notice or dialogue with six Dutch comprehension questions, and one free-production request. Required callouts can cover grammar, formal register, a typical reflexive error and when to ask a staff member to repeat something.

**Off-limits:** treatment, diagnosis, emergency triage, exact healthcare or municipal eligibility rules, and a large reflexive-verb catalogue. Do not treat a classroom role-play as a new event in Sofia's biography.

## Row 39 — Messages, emails and conversations

**Target:** connect short clauses into a coherent message and choose a suitable informal or formal register.

**Story and continuity:** Wednesday 27 January 2027. Sofia reviews the service role-plays with Noor and writes two clearly labelled practice messages: a short informal note to a friend about an ordinary delay or question, and a formal request to a service desk about a form. They are model messages, not evidence that she actually missed an appointment or submitted a request. A dialogue can show Noor helping Sofia revise the tone and word order; a second can be a short spoken exchange about the message.

**Grammar facts:**
- Recycle coordination with *en, maar, of* and *want* (main-clause order), and subordination with *omdat, dat* and temporal/conditional *als* (verb-final order). Choose links by meaning; do not use *want* and *omdat* as if their syntax were the same.
- Add a bounded new clause link: *terwijl* for simultaneous situations. Teach its verb-final clause and a clear, present-time example. For an indirect yes/no question introduce *of* meaning “whether” in a useful message frame (*Ik wil graag weten of …*), clearly distinct from *of* meaning “or”. Keep direct-to-indirect questions short; do not generalise every indirect-question word.
- Show message structure: greeting, reason, request or answer, closing. Contrast *Hoi … / Groetjes* with *Beste … / Met vriendelijke groet* and use *je* or *u* consistently. One connected paragraph can mix already taught clause types while remaining readable at early A2.

**Vocabulary:** roughly 15–25 new terms/chunks for communication and correspondence, checked against prior lessons. Reuse the known *bericht*, *e-mail* and service words where already carded; card a new phrase only for a genuine new use.

**Practice:** pick a link by meaning and word order; complete *terwijl* and *of* clauses; repair punctuation or verb placement; change tone for the recipient; order sentences into a message; translations both ways; read a short email thread with six or more Dutch questions; write a 4–6 sentence informal or formal model message with an author's version. The writing task should receive useful notes about register and alternative wording.

**Off-limits:** a survey of advanced conjunctions (*hoewel, nadat, voordat*) or embedded questions beyond the bounded *of* frame, long official forms, legal advice, and a real submission or reply that changes the story.

## Row 40 — Telling your story

**Target:** integrate main and subordinate clause word order in a short account of past events, present life and a plausible next step.

**Story and continuity:** Friday 29 January 2027. Sofia works on a personal account for Marieke's class and talks it through with Noor. She recalls established events: arriving in Utrecht, living with Noor, moving to Kastanjestraat 14, making friends, working and studying Dutch, visiting Valencia at Christmas, and returning in January. Do not add a new trip, job result, relationship change, or fixed future commitment. End with Sofia able to tell this story and ask for help in Dutch; avoid claiming she has mastered the language.

**Grammar facts:**
- Recycle present, perfect and selected simple past from Part V, plus a modest future statement from row 36. Keep tense choice tied to the story's time frame; do not teach a new tense.
- Bring together main-clause V2, inversion after a fronted time/place or subordinate clause, and verb-final clauses with *dat, omdat, als, terwijl* or indirect *of* when their meanings fit. Recycle a subject relative *die/dat* only in a short, transparent description. Ask learners to see one clear clause boundary at a time.
- Use sequencing words already taught (*eerst, toen, daarna, uiteindelijk*) without treating *toen* as a newly taught subordinate conjunction. Allow short sentences; a good story need not pack every rule into one sentence.

**Vocabulary:** mostly recycle Parts I–VII. New words should be few, useful and checked for first occurrence; do not fill a quota with abstract storytelling terms. Include New words blocks for any genuinely new terms introduced.

**Practice:** order and combine story sentences, choose tense by context, repair V2 and subordinate verb order, transform a fronted clause, complete a dialogue, translate both ways, read Sofia's account with at least six Dutch questions, and write a connected 5–7 sentence personal account with a model answer. Meet the part-wide volume and feature criteria through meaningful integration, including two dialogues and ten translated examples. An independent reviewer checks that every recalled story fact and date agrees with the ledgers and storyline.

**Off-limits:** passive voice, object/prepositional relative clauses, counterfactual past, unfamiliar advanced conjunctions, and new biography invented to make the finale dramatic.
